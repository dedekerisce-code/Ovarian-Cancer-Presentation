01 / INTRODUCTION

What Is Ovarian Cancer?

Ovarian cancer is a disease characterised by the uncontrolled growth of abnormal cells in or around the ovaries.

These abnormal cells can multiply, form tumours and invade surrounding tissues. In some cases, cancer cells may spread to distant parts of the body.

The disease is often difficult to detect in its early stages because symptoms may be subtle or resemble common conditions.

01 / INTRODUCTION

Three Main Types.

Ovarian cancers are classified according to the cells from which they originate.

01

Epithelial Tumours

Develop from cells covering the outer surface of the ovary or related tissues. These account for the majority of ovarian cancers.

MOST COMMON
02

Germ Cell Tumours

Develop from the reproductive cells responsible for producing eggs. They are more frequently diagnosed in younger individuals.

LESS COMMON
03

Sex Cord-Stromal Tumours

Develop from connective and hormone-producing tissues within the ovary.

LESS COMMON

02 / RISK FACTORS

What Increases the Risk?

Several factors may influence an individual's likelihood of developing ovarian cancer.

01

Age

Risk increases with age, particularly after menopause.

02

Family History

A history of ovarian, breast or related cancers may increase risk.

03

Inherited Mutations

Changes in genes such as BRCA1 and BRCA2 can increase susceptibility.

04

Reproductive Factors

Factors affecting lifetime ovulation may influence risk.

05

Endometriosis

Some forms of endometriosis are associated with increased risk of certain ovarian cancers.

06

Personal Medical History

Previous breast or colorectal cancer may be relevant to an individual's risk assessment.

Having a risk factor does not mean that a person will develop ovarian cancer.

RISK AND SUSCEPTIBILITY 05 / 26

03 / DISEASE DEVELOPMENT

How Does It Develop?

Ovarian cancer develops when genetic changes disrupt the normal regulation of cell growth and division.

01

Genetic Changes

Mutations affect genes responsible for controlling cell growth, DNA repair and cell survival.

02

Abnormal Cell Growth

Cells begin dividing uncontrollably and may avoid normal mechanisms of cell death.

03

Tumour Formation

Accumulating abnormal cells can form a tumour within or around the ovary.

04

Invasion and Spread

Cancer cells may invade nearby tissues and spread through the abdominal cavity or bloodstream.

04 / CLINICAL PRESENTATION

Early vs. Advanced Symptoms.

EARLY PRESENTATION

Subtle & Non-specific

  • Mild abdominal discomfort
  • Occasional bloating
  • Changes in appetite
  • Subtle urinary symptoms

Early symptoms may be mild, intermittent or mistaken for common digestive or urinary conditions.

ADVANCED PRESENTATION

Persistent & Widespread

  • Increasing abdominal swelling
  • Persistent pelvic or abdominal pain
  • Significant appetite changes
  • Fatigue and weight changes

Symptoms may become more pronounced due to tumour growth, fluid accumulation or spread to other tissues.

Symptoms do not follow a fixed sequence, and early-stage disease may also cause significant symptoms.

05 / STAGING & PROGRESSION

Stages of Ovarian Cancer.

Staging describes how far the cancer has spread from its original site.

I

Confined

Cancer is limited to one or both ovaries or fallopian tubes.

II

Pelvic Spread

Cancer has spread to other structures within the pelvis.

III

Abdominal Spread

Cancer has spread beyond the pelvis into the abdominal cavity and/or regional lymph nodes.

IV

Distant Spread

Cancer has spread to distant organs or sites outside the abdominal cavity.

The stage is determined through clinical evaluation, imaging, surgery and pathological findings.

UNDERSTANDING THE EXTENT OF DISEASE 09 / 26

05 / STAGING

I

Stage I

Cancer is confined to the ovaries or fallopian tubes.

Stage IA

Cancer is present in one ovary or one fallopian tube, with the capsule intact and no cancer cells detected in the surrounding fluid.

Stage IB

Cancer is present in both ovaries or both fallopian tubes, with the relevant capsules intact.

Stage IC

Cancer is limited to the ovaries or fallopian tubes, but additional risk features are present, such as surgical spill, capsule rupture, surface tumour or malignant cells in abdominal fluid.

SYMPTOM PRESENTATION

Some individuals experience no noticeable symptoms. Others may have mild bloating, pelvic discomfort or changes in appetite.

II

Stage II

Cancer has spread to structures within the pelvis.

Stage IIA

Cancer has spread to or implanted on the uterus, fallopian tubes or other ovarian structures within the pelvis.

Stage IIB

Cancer has spread to other pelvic intraperitoneal tissues, such as the bladder or bowel surface.

SYMPTOM PRESENTATION

Symptoms may include persistent pelvic pain, abdominal discomfort, bloating, urinary changes or changes in bowel habits.

05 / STAGING

III

Stage III

Cancer has spread beyond the pelvis into the abdominal cavity and/or regional lymph nodes.

Stage IIIA

Microscopic spread to the peritoneum outside the pelvis and/or involvement of retroperitoneal lymph nodes.

Stage IIIB

Visible deposits of cancer on the peritoneum outside the pelvis measuring up to 2 cm.

Stage IIIC

Larger peritoneal deposits measuring more than 2 cm, including spread to the surface of the liver or spleen.

SYMPTOM PRESENTATION

Symptoms may include abdominal swelling, ascites, pain, digestive difficulties, early satiety, fatigue and unexplained weight changes.

05 / STAGING

IV

Stage IV

Cancer has spread to distant sites beyond the abdominal cavity.

Stage IVA

Cancer cells are found in the fluid surrounding the lungs, confirmed through positive cytology of the pleural fluid.

Stage IVB

Cancer has spread to distant organs or sites, such as the liver or lung tissue, or lymph nodes outside the abdominal cavity.

SYMPTOM PRESENTATION

Symptoms depend on the sites affected and may include significant fatigue, weight loss, breathing difficulties, persistent pain and reduced appetite.

05 / STAGING & PROGRESSION

Progression of Disease.

As ovarian cancer advances, malignant cells may spread through several pathways.

01

Local Growth

Cancer cells multiply within the ovary or fallopian tube.

02

Local Invasion

Tumour cells invade nearby pelvic structures and tissues.

03

Peritoneal Spread

Cancer cells may detach and spread across surfaces within the abdominal cavity.

04

Distant Metastasis

Cancer cells may spread to distant organs or sites through lymphatic or blood circulation.

The pattern and speed of progression vary between individuals and cancer subtypes.

06 / COMPLICATIONS

Possible Complications.

Ovarian cancer and its progression may interfere with the normal functioning of nearby organs.

01

Ascites

Abnormal accumulation of fluid within the abdominal cavity, causing swelling and discomfort.

02

Bowel Obstruction

Tumour growth or abdominal spread may interfere with the movement of intestinal contents.

03

Urinary Obstruction

Pressure from tumours may obstruct the urinary tract and affect kidney function.

04

Pleural Effusion

Fluid may accumulate around the lungs, potentially causing shortness of breath.

05

Organ Damage

Advanced disease may affect the function of organs involved in tumour spread or compression.

COMPLICATIONS / 01 15 / 26

06 / COMPLICATIONS

Additional Complications.

06

Malnutrition & Cachexia

Reduced appetite and changes in metabolism may contribute to weight loss and muscle wasting.

07

Anaemia

Low red blood cell levels may result from the disease, bleeding or treatment.

08

Venous Thromboembolism

Cancer can increase the risk of blood clots forming in deep veins or travelling to the lungs.

09

Infertility

The disease and some treatments may affect reproductive function and fertility.

10

Recurrence

Cancer may return after treatment, requiring further monitoring and management.

07 / DIAGNOSIS

How Is It Diagnosed?

Diagnosis involves a combination of clinical assessment, imaging and laboratory investigations.

01

Clinical Assessment

Medical history, symptoms and physical examination.

02

Imaging

Ultrasound, CT or MRI to assess abnormal growths and possible spread.

03

Blood Tests

Assessment of tumour markers and other relevant laboratory findings.

04

Tissue Examination

Pathological examination confirms the cancer type and characteristics.

No single blood test or imaging study can independently confirm every case of ovarian cancer.

THE DIAGNOSTIC PROCESS 17 / 26

07 / DIAGNOSIS

Examination & Imaging.

Pelvic Examination

A healthcare professional assesses the pelvic organs for abnormalities or unusual masses.

Ultrasound

Transvaginal or abdominal ultrasound can help identify ovarian masses and examine their size and appearance.

CT & MRI Scans

These imaging techniques help evaluate tumour extent, surrounding structures and possible metastatic spread.

07 / DIAGNOSIS

Markers & Tissue Diagnosis.

CA-125

CA-125 is a protein that may be elevated in the blood of some individuals with epithelial ovarian cancer.

However, elevated levels can also occur in non-cancerous conditions, while some ovarian cancers do not cause elevated CA-125 levels.

Histopathology

Examination of tissue under a microscope helps establish the diagnosis and identify the specific cancer type.

Additional Markers

Other tests may be selected depending on the suspected tumour type and clinical findings.

08 / GENETIC FACTORS

BRCA1 & BRCA2.

Two important genes involved in maintaining genomic stability and repairing damaged DNA.

GENE 01

BRCA1

Produces a protein that helps repair damaged DNA and maintain the stability of genetic material.

KEY FUNCTION DNA DAMAGE REPAIR
GENE 02

BRCA2

Helps repair DNA double-strand breaks through homologous recombination, an important high-fidelity repair pathway.

KEY FUNCTION HOMOLOGOUS RECOMBINATION

Harmful inherited variants in either gene can significantly increase the risk of developing ovarian and certain other cancers.

08 / GENETIC FACTORS

Lynch Syndrome & Other Genes.

Lynch Syndrome

Lynch syndrome is an inherited condition caused by pathogenic variants in genes involved in DNA mismatch repair.

It increases the risk of several cancers, including colorectal, endometrial and ovarian cancer.

Mismatch Repair Genes

Important genes include MLH1, MSH2, MSH6 and PMS2. EPCAM deletions can also contribute to Lynch syndrome.

Other Susceptibility Genes

Additional genes, including RAD51C, RAD51D and BRIP1, are associated with increased ovarian cancer susceptibility.

08 / GENETIC FACTORS

Inherited vs. Acquired Mutations.

GERMLINE MUTATIONS

Inherited

  • Present from birth
  • Usually found in nearly all body cells
  • Can be passed from parent to child
  • May increase inherited cancer susceptibility
SOMATIC MUTATIONS

Acquired

  • Develop during a person's lifetime
  • Occur in particular cells or tissues
  • Are not usually passed to children
  • Can contribute to tumour development

Both inherited and acquired mutations can influence ovarian cancer development and treatment decisions.

09 / TREATMENT

Surgery & Chemotherapy.

TREATMENT APPROACH 01

Surgery

Surgery aims to remove the tumour and as much visible cancer as possible.

  • Removal of affected ovaries and fallopian tubes
  • Possible removal of the uterus
  • Removal of visible tumour deposits when appropriate
  • Surgical staging to determine disease extent
TREATMENT APPROACH 02

Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy uses anti-cancer drugs to destroy cancer cells or prevent them from dividing.

  • May be given before or after surgery
  • Commonly uses platinum-based drugs
  • Often combined with other chemotherapy agents
  • May help reduce tumour burden and recurrence risk
CONVENTIONAL TREATMENT 23 / 26

09 / TREATMENT

Targeted Therapy.

Targeted treatments act on specific molecular pathways or weaknesses within cancer cells.

MOLECULAR TARGETING
01 / PARP INHIBITORS

Exploiting DNA Repair Defects

PARP inhibitors interfere with DNA repair. They can be effective in selected cancers with BRCA mutations or other homologous recombination repair deficiencies.

02 / ANGIOGENESIS INHIBITORS

Limiting Blood Supply

Medicines such as bevacizumab target vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), reducing the formation of new blood vessels that support tumour growth.

03 / BIOMARKER-BASED CARE

Personalised Treatment

Genetic and molecular testing can help identify patients who may benefit from particular targeted therapies.
